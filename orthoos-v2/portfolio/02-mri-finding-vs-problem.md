# 2. MRI finding vs actual problem — the degenerative meniscus

*Synthetic case. No real patient data. Author: Dr. Aman Khanna, orthopaedic
surgeon (arthroscopy, sports medicine).*

## Why this piece exists

The most reliable failure mode I see in AI answers about knee MRI is
treating the report as the diagnosis. Imaging-adjacent AI — and the
patients who paste their reports into chatbots — need evaluators who know
which findings explain symptoms and which are incidental. This is that
distinction, worked through one canonical case.

## Synthetic case

A 52-year-old shopkeeper has three months of medial knee pain, worse on
stairs and after sitting. No injury. No locking, no giving-way. Examination:
mild medial joint-line tenderness, full extension, flexion 130°, no
effusion, stable ligaments. Standing X-ray: early medial compartment
narrowing. MRI report: *"Complex tear of the posterior horn of the medial
meniscus. Grade 2–3 chondral changes, medial compartment."*

The patient arrives asking: **"My meniscus is torn — when is the operation?"**

## The gap between the sentence and the knee

- Degenerative meniscal tears are common incidental findings on MRI in
  people over 50, including in knees with no pain at all. Their prevalence
  rises with age and with coexisting osteoarthritic change — exactly this
  patient's picture.
- This knee's actual problem is **early medial compartment osteoarthritis**;
  the meniscal tear is part of that same degenerative process, not a
  discrete injury awaiting repair.
- Randomised trials — including sham-surgery-controlled work — have shown
  no meaningful advantage for arthroscopic partial meniscectomy over
  structured exercise therapy in degenerative tears without mechanical
  locking. Operating on the report sentence, rather than the knee,
  is the error the evidence warns against.

## What correct management looks like here

First-line: education (this is the load-bearing step — the patient came in
expecting surgery), analgesia strategy, quadriceps and hip-abductor
strengthening, weight optimisation, activity modification; review at 8–12
weeks. Arthroscopy is reconsidered only for true mechanical symptoms
(a locked knee, reproducible painful catching) — not for pain alone.
The conversation, honestly had, is: "surgery does not fix this kind of
tear better than rehabilitation does."

## The grading checkpoints this case yields

1. **Incidentaloma awareness** — did the answer say that this MRI finding is
   common in asymptomatic knees of this age?
2. **Correct problem naming** — did it identify early osteoarthritis as the
   primary diagnosis rather than "torn meniscus"?
3. **Evidence-aligned management order** — exercise-first, surgery gated on
   mechanical symptoms; an answer recommending meniscectomy for this
   presentation is an automatic fail.
4. **Expectation repair** — did it address the patient's stated belief
   ("torn = operate") directly? An answer can be factually right and still
   fail the patient by leaving that belief intact.
